# Chapter 22: Maintaining Your System Long-Term

Building a system is an event. Maintaining a system is a practice. This distinction is where ADHD management most commonly and most painfully fails. The energy and motivation of a new system — the relief of having a plan, the novelty of new tools and new approaches — are exactly the kinds of activation that the ADHD interest-based nervous system provides generously at the beginning of any project. Maintaining a system two months later, when it is no longer novel and the initial relief has faded, requires something different: deliberate architecture for long-term sustainability.

## The ADHD System Collapse Cycle

The cycle is familiar: build a system with genuine commitment and initial success, maintain it through the initial motivation period, encounter a disruption (illness, travel, deadline crisis, relationship stress), allow one small system failure, experience the shame of that failure as evidence that you 'can't stick with anything,' abandon the system entirely rather than repair the small failure, and live in chaos until the pain drives another rebuild. This cycle is not a character flaw. It is an entirely predictable consequence of ADHD's sensitivity to shame and its difficulty with the long-horizon perspective needed to repair rather than abandon.

## The Weekly Review as Engine of Maintenance

The weekly review is the single most important maintenance practice in any ADHD management system. Borrowed from David Allen's Getting Things Done methodology and adapted for ADHD, the weekly review is a structured 30-45 minute session that processes the previous week, prepares for the coming week, and repairs any system components that have drifted. Its regularity prevents the accumulation of unprocessed items that eventually overwhelms the system and triggers abandonment. Its structure — a fixed agenda, completed at a fixed time, in a fixed location — makes it a habit rather than a decision.

## ADHD Coaching and Accountability

The evidence base for ADHD coaching has grown substantially in recent years. Swartz and colleagues (2005) documented the effectiveness of structured support interventions for ADHD, and subsequent research has supported the value of regular accountability check-ins in maintaining ADHD management systems. An ADHD coach provides what the ADHD brain most reliably lacks: consistent external accountability, a structured review process, and a compassionate witness to both progress and failure who does not live in the house and does not carry the history of past system collapses.

## The Minimum Viable System

One of the most important ADHD system maintenance principles is the minimum viable system: when life becomes overwhelming and the full system becomes unsustainable, shrink to the irreducible core rather than abandoning everything. Identify the three behaviors that, if maintained, would prevent total system collapse and allow recovery: perhaps a daily capture habit, a weekly review, and medication adherence. These three behaviors become the minimum viable system — the floor below which you will not fall, regardless of what else is happening.

Carol Dweck's foundational work on growth mindset, published in her 2006 book Mindset, provides the psychological framework for maintenance: abilities and systems develop through effort and learning — they are not fixed traits that one either has or does not. Applied to ADHD system maintenance, this means every collapse is data, not verdict. The question is never 'Why can't I stick to anything?' The question is: 'What specifically failed, and how does this system need to be redesigned to prevent that exact failure next time?' Failure is the R&D; of a system that is still being refined. The only true failure is abandonment.

## KEY INSIGHT

A perfect system abandoned is worth nothing. An imperfect system maintained is everything. The goal is not a flawless system that works when conditions are ideal. The goal is a resilient system that survives the real conditions of an ADHD life — that can be partially disrupted and repaired without being abandoned, that can be shrunk to a minimum viable version and rebuilt from there.

## YOUR TURN

1. Design your minimum viable system: identify the three behaviors that, if maintained through any disruption, would prevent total collapse and allow recovery. Write them down. Post them where you can see them.

2. Set a weekly review appointment in your calendar for the next twelve weeks. Make it recurring, at a specific time, in a specific location, with a specific agenda. Protect it as you would protect a medical appointment.

3. Identify one accountability partner — a friend, colleague, coach, or therapist — who will check in on your system maintenance weekly for the next month. Ask them this week.

4. Review the last time your ADHD system collapsed. What specifically triggered the failure? What was the smallest repair that would have prevented full abandonment? Redesign for that specific failure point.
