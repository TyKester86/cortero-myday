# Chapter 4: Medication — What the Research Actually Says

Few topics in ADHD generate as much confusion, stigma, and misinformation as medication.

Stimulant medications are simultaneously among the most effective psychiatric medications ever studied and among the most politically contested. This chapter presents the evidence base clearly and dispassionately — what the research actually shows about efficacy, mechanism, limitations, and the appropriate role of medication in comprehensive ADHD management.

## Stimulant Medications

Two classes of stimulant medication dominate the treatment of ADHD: methylphenidate-based medications (including Ritalin, Concerta, Metadate, and Focalin) and amphetamine-based medications (including Adderall, Vyvanse, and Dexedrine). Both classes work by blocking the reuptake of dopamine and norepinephrine in synaptic clefts — increasing the availability of these neurotransmitters in the prefrontal cortex and normalizing executive function. They differ in their precise mechanisms, duration of action, pharmacokinetics, and individual response profiles.

The effect sizes of stimulant medications for ADHD are among the largest in all of psychiatry. Meta-analyses consistently find Cohen's d values of 0.8 to 1.0 for symptom reduction — effect sizes that place stimulants in the category of 'large effect' by conventional standards, comparable to antibiotics for bacterial infections in terms of the consistency and magnitude of their impact. Cortese and colleagues' landmark network meta-analysis (2018) in The Lancet Psychiatry, synthesizing evidence from 133 randomized controlled trials involving more than 24,000 participants across all age groups, confirmed that stimulant medications are the most effective pharmacological intervention for ADHD in both children and adults.

## Non-Stimulant Options

For individuals who cannot tolerate stimulants, who have contraindications, or who need additional symptom coverage beyond stimulant hours, non-stimulant medications offer meaningful alternatives. Atomoxetine (Strattera) is a selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor — the first non-stimulant approved specifically for ADHD. It has no abuse potential, works continuously rather than in timed doses, and is particularly effective for patients with co-occurring anxiety. Its limitations include a slower onset (4-8 weeks for full effect) and generally smaller effect sizes than stimulants.

Viloxazine (Qelbree) is a newer non-stimulant approved in 2021, mechanistically similar to atomoxetine with some serotonergic activity that may benefit co-occurring depression and anxiety. Guanfacine (Intuniv) and Clonidine (Kapvay) are alpha-2 adrenergic agonists that directly modulate noradrenergic signaling in the prefrontal cortex. They are particularly effective for hyperactivity, impulsivity, and emotional dysregulation, and are frequently used as adjuncts to stimulants or in populations (young children, individuals with tics) where stimulants are problematic.

## Addressing the Stigma

The stigma surrounding ADHD medication is both pervasive and scientifically untenable. Barkley's analogy is precise and worth reproducing in full: we would never tell a person with diabetes that managing their insulin through willpower rather than insulin therapy is the appropriate treatment. We would never shame a person with myopia for using glasses rather than 'trying harder to see.' ADHD medication is not a crutch. It is a therapeutic intervention for a documented neurological condition, prescribed by a physician, monitored carefully, and adjusted based on individual response. The concern about stimulant 'performance enhancement' in individuals without ADHD is legitimate and worth distinguishing from therapeutic use. Research consistently shows that stimulants produce larger and more consistent effects in individuals with ADHD than in neurotypical individuals — reflecting the underlying neurological difference that creates the therapeutic need.

## What Medication Does Not Fix

This section is as important as any in this chapter. Medication is a tool with a defined scope of action. It reduces ADHD symptoms — it improves inhibition, working memory, and attention. It does not install habits. It does not create organizational systems. It does not repair relationships damaged by years of ADHD behavior. It does not eliminate time blindness entirely. It does not resolve the sleep dysregulation that is endemic in ADHD. And it does not work when it wears off — typically in the evening, which is precisely when many adults with ADHD most need their executive function for family life, personal organization, and sleep preparation.

The most effective ADHD management combines medication and behavioral systems. Medication opens the window — it creates the neurological conditions in which executive function is more accessible. Behavioral systems do the work: the routines, the environmental design, the external accountability structures, and the habit architecture that this book describes. Neither alone achieves what both together can produce.

## KEY INSIGHT

Medication is a tool, not a cure. Combined with behavioral systems, it produces outcomes that neither achieves alone. Choosing medication is a medical decision, not a moral one. Choosing not to medicate is also valid — but it increases the weight that behavioral systems must carry.
