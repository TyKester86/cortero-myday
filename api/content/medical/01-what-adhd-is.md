# What ADHD (and "ADD") is — and isn't

### What ADHD is
summary: ADHD (attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder) is one of the most common neurodevelopmental disorders of childhood. It involves trouble with attention, controlling impulses and activity level. It usually starts in childhood and often lasts into adulthood. It is a recognized medical condition, not a character flaw or a parenting failure.
source: CDC — About ADHD (last updated June 1, 2026)
url: https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/about/index.html

### NIMH's definition
summary: The National Institute of Mental Health describes ADHD as a neurodevelopmental disorder marked by inattention (trouble paying attention, staying on task or staying organized), hyperactivity (moving around a lot, feeling restless, talking a lot) and impulsivity (interrupting, intruding, trouble waiting a turn). Symptoms begin in childhood and usually continue into the teen years and adulthood.
source: National Institute of Mental Health — Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (revised August 2026)
url: https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-adhd

### The three presentations (the "types")
summary: ADHD shows up in three ways. Predominantly inattentive: mostly trouble focusing, organizing and finishing tasks. Predominantly hyperactive-impulsive: mostly fidgeting, talking a lot, trouble sitting still and acting without thinking. Combined: both. A person's presentation can change over time.
source: CDC — About ADHD (last updated June 1, 2026)
url: https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/about/index.html

### "ADD" is the old name
summary: "ADD" (attention deficit disorder) is the name the diagnostic manual used in 1980 (DSM-III), when it was described "with or without hyperactivity." In 1987 (DSM-III-R) it was renamed ADHD, and since 1994 (DSM-IV) a purely inattentive form has been part of ADHD as the "predominantly inattentive" type. So what people call ADD today is ADHD, predominantly inattentive presentation — not a separate condition.
source: Lange KW et al. — The history of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder. Atten Defic Hyperact Disord 2(4):241–255 (December 2010)
url: https://doi.org/10.1007/s12402-010-0045-8

### Known risk factors
summary: Researchers have identified several risk factors: genetics; exposure to environmental risks such as lead during pregnancy or early childhood; alcohol and tobacco use during pregnancy; brain injuries and some health conditions in childhood; parental mental health problems; and the family environment. Based on dozens of twin studies, an international consensus statement concludes that genes and their interaction with the environment play a substantial role in causing ADHD.
source: CDC — About ADHD (last updated June 1, 2026); Faraone SV et al. — The World Federation of ADHD International Consensus Statement, Neurosci Biobehav Rev 128:789–818 (September 2021)
url: https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/about/index.html

### What does NOT cause ADHD: sugar
summary: A meta-analysis of double-blind studies found that sugar does not affect the behavior or thinking of children; parents' strong belief that it does may come from expectation. A small effect in some children cannot be completely ruled out. A large international consensus statement also found no evidence of a link between sugar and ADHD.
source: Wolraich ML et al. — The effect of sugar on behavior or cognition in children: a meta-analysis. JAMA 274(20):1617–1621 (November 1995); Faraone SV et al. — The World Federation of ADHD International Consensus Statement, Neurosci Biobehav Rev 128:789–818 (September 2021)
url: https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.1995.03530200053037

### How common it is in U.S. children
summary: In 2024 about 7 million U.S. children aged 3–17 (11.7%) had a current ADHD diagnosis. Boys (13%) are diagnosed more often than girls (7%) — though girls are often missed (see the women and girls entries).
source: CDC — Data and Statistics on ADHD (last updated July 8, 2026)
url: https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/data/index.html

### How common it is worldwide
summary: Across studies from every region, about 5.9% of young people meet criteria for ADHD, with no meaningful differences between continents, and roughly 2.5% of adults. In young people about two boys are diagnosed for every girl.
source: Faraone SV et al. — The World Federation of ADHD International Consensus Statement: 208 Evidence-based conclusions about the disorder. Neurosci Biobehav Rev 128:789–818 (September 2021)
url: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2021.01.022
