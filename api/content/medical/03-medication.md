# Medication: what the evidence says

Hana never recommends, starts, stops or changes anyone's medication or dose. These entries explain what the guidelines and research say so people can have better conversations with their prescriber.

### Stimulants and non-stimulants
summary: Two kinds of medicine are FDA-approved for ADHD. Stimulants are the best known and most widely used: 70–80% of children with ADHD have fewer symptoms on these fast-acting medicines. Non-stimulants (approved for ADHD in 2003) work more slowly, but their effect can last up to 24 hours. Medicines can have side effects such as trouble sleeping and loss of appetite, so the prescriber watches closely and adjusts the dose to balance benefits and side effects.
source: CDC — Treatment of ADHD (last updated June 2, 2026)
url: https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/treatment/index.html

### Treatment by age (U.S. guidance)
summary: For children under 6, parent training in behavior management is the first treatment, before medication is tried. For children 6 and older, medication and behavior therapy are recommended together. The American Academy of Pediatrics adds: ages 4–6, behavioral treatment first and methylphenidate only if that isn't enough and moderate-to-severe problems continue; ages 6–12, FDA-approved medicine plus parent training and/or classroom behavioral help (preferably both) and school supports such as an IEP or 504 plan; ages 12–18, medicine with the teen's agreement plus behavioral help and school supports. Doses are adjusted to get the most benefit with side effects the child can tolerate.
source: CDC — Treatment of ADHD (last updated June 2, 2026); Wolraich ML et al., American Academy of Pediatrics — Clinical Practice Guideline, Pediatrics 144(4):e20192528 (October 2019)
url: https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/treatment/index.html

### First choices of medicine (UK guidance)
summary: NICE recommends methylphenidate as the first medicine for children 5 and over and young people, with lisdexamfetamine considered after a 6-week trial that didn't help enough. For adults, lisdexamfetamine or methylphenidate are first-line, with switching between them after an adequate 6-week trial, and atomoxetine (a non-stimulant) if neither works or both can't be tolerated. Medication should only be started by a professional with ADHD expertise. No child under 5 should be given ADHD medication without a second specialist opinion.
source: NICE — Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder: diagnosis and management (NG87), published 14 March 2018, last updated 13 September 2019
url: https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87

### Which medicines work best (largest comparison)
summary: The biggest comparison of ADHD medicines (133 double-blind trials, about 10,000 children and 8,000 adults) found all of them better than placebo in children by clinicians' ratings. Weighing benefit and side effects together, it supported methylphenidate as the preferred first choice for children and teens, and amphetamines for adults, for short-term treatment. The trials were mostly about 12 weeks long; there wasn't enough data on longer-term effects.
source: Cortese S et al. — Comparative efficacy and tolerability of medications for ADHD in children, adolescents, and adults: a systematic review and network meta-analysis. Lancet Psychiatry 5(9):727–738 (September 2018)
url: https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(18)30269-4

### Benefits beyond symptoms, and safety
summary: An international consensus of 80 experts concluded that ADHD medicines are safe and effective at reducing symptoms; that treatment is linked to fewer accidental injuries, traumatic brain injuries, substance use problems, smoking, school underachievement, bone fractures, depression, suicide, criminal activity and teen pregnancy; that side effects are typically mild and can usually be handled by changing the dose or the medicine; and that stimulants work better than non-stimulants but are more likely to be misused or given to others.
source: Faraone SV et al. — The World Federation of ADHD International Consensus Statement: 208 Evidence-based conclusions about the disorder. Neurosci Biobehav Rev 128:789–818 (September 2021)
url: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2021.01.022
